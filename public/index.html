MethodHow the calls are made
  1. Surprise. For each release, actual − forecast, normalised by that indicator's own historical surprise volatility to a z-score.
  2. Learned response. Per event type and currency we regress the post-release return (in basis points) on the surprise. The fitted slope is the edge — measured, not assumed.
  3. Gate. A release only becomes a candidate at |z| ≥ 2, where the backtest shows the edge begins. Below that it is noise.
  4. Follow or fade. Ordinary surprises are traded with the measured response; very large ones (|z| ≥ 2.5) tend to over-react, so they are faded instead. The backtest decides which is used.
  5. Instruments. 28 FX crosses plus gold (XAU/USD) and bitcoin (BTC/USD), driven by the USD response and scored on their own price series.
  6. Honest scoring. Walk-forward throughout: a call for year Y only uses earlier data. Confidence is shrunk toward a coin flip when the sample is thin, and the sample size is always shown.

What this is not: a 70%-accurate crystal ball. Intraday post-release trading of liquid majors is close to efficient, and no honest model gets 70% of daily FX calls right. The durable edge is modest — about 54% overall, rising to ~62% on calls whose bucket has a proven, large sample — and it concentrates in large surprises and a few event types (rate decisions, housing, inflation). Small surprises are a coin flip, and the app says so rather than dressing them up.

Not financial advice. Statistical estimates from historical data; they can be wrong. A backtest is not a promise.
